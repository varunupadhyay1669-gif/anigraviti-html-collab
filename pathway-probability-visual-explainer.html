Pathways, Splits, and Different Bins

This page is built to spend most of its attention on the question itself: what the words mean, how the pathway machine behaves, and what "different bins" really asks us to compare. Only after that do we solve it.

Question-first explanation Mind map included Answer grows from the visuals

The Original Question

A network of pathways lead from a single opening to three bins, labelled A, B, C. If a ball is dropped into the opening, it will follow a path and land in one of the bins. Every time a path splits, it is equally likely for the ball to follow either of the downward paths. Ellen drops two balls, one after the other, into the opening. What is the probability that the two balls land in different bins?

Question translated into student language

Each ball is making a chain of left-right decisions. We do not care about the exact route at the end, only which bin the ball finally reaches.

Random part

At each split, the ball has a 50% chance of going each way.

Important goal

Compare the two final bins, not the full routes the balls took.

Section 1

Decode the question before touching the answer

The student should first see what the machine is doing, what stays fixed, what is random, and what the question is really asking us to notice.

What must the student notice?

1. One ball at a time

The first ball finishes. Then a second ball starts fresh from the top.

2. Every split is fair

Whenever the path branches, the ball chooses left or right with equal probability.

3. Different route lengths can happen

Some balls reach a bin after fewer splits, while others travel longer.

4. Only the bin matters at the end

Two paths can be different but still land in the same final bin.

Turn the words into a thinking sequence

1
Read the machine

See the network as a decision machine that keeps sending the ball downward.

2
Track one ball first

Ask: how likely is one ball to land in A, B, or C?

3
Then bring in the second ball

The second ball follows the same rules, independently of the first ball.

4
Ask the comparison question

Do the two balls end in the same bin or in different bins?

Mind map: what the question is really about

Section 2

See the pathway machine and the comparison idea

This is still the understanding phase. The student should be able to say, "I know what happens to one ball, and I know what must be compared for two balls."

Pathway map of the network

What should the student see here?

There are not equally many routes to each bin. Even though every split is fair, the whole machine is not balanced evenly between A, B, and C. Bin B collects the most route families, so it will turn out to be the most likely.

Reading one ball correctly

A ball does not choose a bin directly. It only chooses at each split. The final bin is the result of a whole chain of choices.

fair split downward motion some routes are longer

Why B should feel important before solving

Look at the map: several different branches can still funnel back into B. This means B is a "collector bin" in the network.

That is a visual clue that the probabilities of A, B, and C are not equal.

What changes when the second ball is dropped?

Nothing about the machine changes. The second ball just repeats the same experiment. That is why we can use the one-ball probabilities twice.

What does "different bins" really mean?

Imagine the first ball gives the row and the second ball gives the column. The red diagonal means "same bin". The green boxes mean "different bins".

Best solving idea before calculation

Once the student understands the grid, there are two natural ways to solve:

A
Direct way

Find all different-bin outcomes and add them.

B
Smarter way

Find the probability of the same bin first, then subtract from 1.

The second way is cleaner because there are only three same-bin cases: A with A, B with B, and C with C.

Section 3

Now the answer sinks in because the question is clear

We keep this part shorter. The heavy work was understanding the machine; now the calculation can feel natural instead of mysterious.

Bin A

There is only one route family to A: 1 -> 2 -> 3 -> A. So P(A) = 18.

Bin B

B collects many route families, so it becomes the largest probability: P(B) = 58.

Bin C

Three route families lead to C, so P(C) = 14.

Use the "same bin" shortcut

Same-bin cases are A,A, B,B, and C,C. Since the two balls are independent:

P(same) = P(A)^2 + P(B)^2 + P(C)^2

= (18)^2 + (58)^2 + (14)^2

= 164 + 2564 + 464 = 3064 = 1532

P(different) = 1 - 1532 = 1732

A
Final answer: 1732

The correct choice is (A).

Teaching note: if the student first understands why B is visually the "collector bin," the numerical answer feels much more reasonable and much less like a trick.
